\chapter{Introduction}

This chapter introduces the central subject of the book: \emph{ordered
semigroup actions} (OSAs) and \emph{spaces-in-general}.  In a given ordered
semigroup action, I call its semigroup elements \emph{space elements};
the many-object version calls the arrows of the underlying semicategory
\emph{interspaces}. These are the objects of study in the later formulas
for continuity and other topological operations.

For a category of familiar structures and its specified maps, a
\emph{space-in-general} in the categorical sense is a structure equipped
with a chosen representation of its category as a full subcategory of
the arrow category of ordered semicategory actions. The representation
retains every map and therefore every isomorphism. The full definition,
the representation theorem, and the catalogue follow in
Section~\ref{sec-space-in-general-representation}. They cover the kinds
of spaces discussed in~\cite{spaces-in-math}, including Euclidean,
linear, topological, Hilbert, and probability spaces, as well as graphs.
The existence theorem also covers algebraic structures, so the chosen
action and the operations it makes available are important data.

In~\cite{important} I argue that ordered semicategory actions (together with below defined \emph{funcoids}) are as important as groups, with the main distinction that discovery of ordered semicategory action was delayed.

\chapter{Prerequisites}

You need to know about \emph{semigroups} and \emph{semigroup actions} before reading further. If in doubt, consult Wikipedia.

I will denote meet and join on a poset correspondingly as~$\sqcap$ and~$\sqcup$.

I call two elements~$a$ and~$b$ \emph{intersecting} ($a\nasymp b$) when there is a non-least element~$c$ such that $c\sqsubseteq a\land c\sqsubseteq b$. For	 meet-semilattices with meet operation~$\sqcap$ this condition is equivalent to $a\sqcap b$ being non-least element.

I call two elements~$a$ and~$b$ \emph{joining} ($a\equiv b$) when there is no non-greatest element~$c$ such that $c\sqsupseteq a\land c\sqsupseteq b$. For join-semilattices with meet operation~$\sqcup$ this condition is equivalent to $a\sqcup b$ being the greatest element.

I denote $\rsupfun{f}X = \setcond{fx}{x\in X}$.

\chapter{Basic examples}

A topological space is determined by its closure operator.

On a fixed set~$X$, the monotone self-maps of~$\subsets X$ form an ordered
monoid under composition and pointwise inclusion. They act on~$\subsets X$
by evaluation, and each topological closure operator is a space element of
this action. Write $c_X$ and $c_Y$ for the closures of two topological
spaces and $f_!(A)=f[A]$ for direct image along $f:X\to Y$. Then
\[
 f\text{ is continuous}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 f_!\circ c_X\sqsubseteq c_Y\circ f_!.
\]
For the forward implication, $f^{-1}[c_Y(f[A])]$ is closed and contains~$A$,
so it contains $c_X(A)$. For the reverse implication, take $A=f^{-1}[B]$
for any closed $B\subseteq Y$. Then $f[c_X(A)]\subseteq c_Y(f[A])\subseteq B$,
so $c_X(A)\subseteq A$. This is an example of the algebraic continuity
condition used later. Its inequality uses the closure operators directly,
whereas the full embedding in Section~\ref{sec-space-in-general-representation}
represents the entire category of topological spaces.

\chapter{Semicategories}

\begin{defn}
\index{semicategory}A \emph{semicategory} is a directed multigraph
together with a partial binary operation $\circ$ on the set~$\mathcal{M}$ of edges (called the set of \emph{morphisms} in the context of semicategories)
such that $g\circ f$ is defined iff $\Dst f=\Src g$ (for every morphisms
$f$ and $g$) such that
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\Src(g\circ f)=\Src f$ and $\Dst(g\circ f)=\Dst g$ whenever the
composition $g\circ f$ of morphisms $f$ and $g$ is defined.
\item $(h\circ g)\circ f=h\circ(g\circ f)$ whenever compositions in this
equation are defined.
\end{enumerate}
\end{defn}

\begin{defn}
\index{semifunctor}A \emph{semifunctor} is a pair of a function from the set of objects of one semicategory to the set of objects of another semicategory and a function from the set of morphisms of one semicategory to the set of morphisms of that another semicategory (these two functions are denoted by the same letter such as~$\phi$) conforming to the axioms:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\phi(f): \phi(\Src f)\to\phi(\Dst f)$ for every morphism~$f$ of the first semicategory;
\item $\phi(g\circ f)=\phi(g)\circ\phi(f)$ for every composable morphisms~$f$,~$g$ of the first semicategory.
\end{enumerate}
\end{defn}

\begin{note}
A semigroup is essentially a special case of a semicategory (with only one object) and semigroup homomorphism is a semifunctor.
\end{note}

\chapter{Ordered semicategories}

\begin{defn}
\emph{Ordered semicategory} (or \emph{posemicategory}) is
a semicategory together with an order on the set of morphisms conforming to the equality:
\[ x_0\sqsubseteq x_1\land y_0\sqsubseteq y_1\Rightarrow y_0\circ x_0\sqsubseteq y_1\circ x_1. \]
\end{defn}

\chapter{Ordered semigroups}

\begin{defn}
\emph{Ordered semigroup} (or \emph{posemigroup}) is a set together with binary operation~$\circ$ and binary relation~$\sqsubseteq$ on it, conforming both to semigroup axioms and partial order axioms and:
\[ x_0\sqsubseteq x_1\land y_0\sqsubseteq y_1\Rightarrow y_0\circ x_0\sqsubseteq y_1\circ x_1. \]
\end{defn}

Essentially, a posemigroup is just an ordered semicategory with just one object.

In the algebraic formulas of this book I call elements of an ordered
semigroup participating in a specified ordered action \emph{spaces} or
\emph{space elements}. The action is part of the datum. The categorical
definition of a space-in-general is given in
Section~\ref{sec-space-in-general-representation}.

As I shown above, functions (and more generally binary relations) can also be considered as spaces.

\chapter{Ordered semicategory actions}

\begin{defn}
\emph{Semicategory action} (denoted $\supfun{}$) is a semifunctor from a semicategory to the category~$\mathbf{Set}$.
\end{defn}

The category~$\mathbf{Pos}$ is the category whose objects are (small) posets and whose morphisms are order homomorphisms.

\begin{defn}
\emph{Semiordered semicategory action} (denoted $\supfun{}$) is a semifunctor from an ordered semicategory~$\mathcal{C}$ to the category~$\mathbf{Pos}$.
\end{defn}

In components, a semiordered semicategory action assigns a poset
$\supfun{X}$ to each object $X$ and a monotone map
$\supfun{a}:\supfun{X}\to\supfun{Y}$ to each arrow $a:X\to Y$.
The assignment preserves composition. I call the arrows of the
underlying semicategory \emph{semi-interspaces}.

\begin{defn}
\emph{Ordered semicategory action} is a semiordered semicategory action with the property
\begin{enumerate}
\item $a\sqsubseteq b\Rightarrow\supfun{a}x\sqsubseteq\supfun{b}x$ for all $a,b\in\Hom(X,Y)$ and $x\in\supfun{X}$.
\end{enumerate}
In other words, an ordered semicategory action is a (not necessarily strictly) increasing semicategory action (we consider transformations of this action to be ordered pointwise, that is by the product order).

I call morphisms of such a semicategory as \emph{interspaces}.
\end{defn}

Note that this ``inducting'' is an ordered semigroup homomorphism.

\chapter{Ordered semigroup actions}

\begin{defn}
\emph{Curried semiordered semigroup action} on a poset~$\mathfrak{A}$ for an ordered semigroup~$S$ is a function $\supfun{}:S\to(\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{A})$ such that
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\supfun{b\circ a}x = \supfun{b}\supfun{a}x$ for all $a,b\in S$, $x\in\mathfrak{A}$;
$x,y\in\mathfrak{A}$;
\item $a\sqsubseteq b\Rightarrow\supfun{a}x\sqsubseteq \supfun{b}x$ for all $a,b\in S$, $x\in\mathfrak{A}$.
\end{enumerate}
I call elements of such an action \emph{semispaces}.
\end{defn}

\begin{defn}
\emph{Curried ordered semigroup action} on a poset~$\mathfrak{A}$ for an ordered semigroup~$S$ is a function $\supfun{}:S\to(\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{A})$ such that
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\supfun{b\circ a}x = \supfun{b}\supfun{a}x$ for all $a,b\in S$, $x\in\mathfrak{A}$;
$x,y\in\mathfrak{A}$;
\item $a\sqsubseteq b\Rightarrow\supfun{a}x\sqsubseteq \supfun{b}x$ for all $a,b\in S$, $x\in\mathfrak{A}$;
\item $x\sqsubseteq y\Rightarrow\supfun{a}x\sqsubseteq \supfun{a}y$ for all $a\in S$.
\end{enumerate}
I call elements of such an action \emph{spaces}.
\end{defn}

Essentially, an ordered semigroup action is an ordered semicategory action with just one object.

We can order actions componentwise. Then the above axioms simplify to:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\supfun{b\circ a} = \supfun{b}\circ\supfun{a}$ for all $a,b\in S$;
\item $\supfun{}$ is a (not necessarily strictly) increasing;
\item $\supfun{a}$ is a (not necessarily strictly) increasing, for every space~$a$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{defn}
A \emph{functional ordered semicategory action} is such an ordered semicategory action that $\supfun{a}=a$ for every space~$a$.
\end{defn}

\begin{thm}
Each ordered semicategory action induces as functional ordered semicategory action, whose morphisms are the same a of the original one but with objects being posets, spaces are the actions of the original semicategory, the composition operation is function composition, and order of spaces is the product order.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
That it's a semicategory is obvious. The partial order is the same as the original. It remains to prove the remaining axioms.

For our semicategory
\[ \supfun{b\circ a} = b\circ a=\supfun{b}\circ \supfun{a}. \]

$\supfun{}$ is increasing because it's the identity function.

$\supfun{a}$ is the same as one of the original ordered semicategory action and thus is increasing.
\end{proof}

\emph{Funcoids} form an ordered semicategory with action $\supfun{}$. \emph{Reloids} form an ordered semicategory with action $a\mapsto\supfun{\tofcd a}$.
As we know from the above, funcoids are a generalization of topological spaces, proximity spaces, and directed graphs (``discrete spaces''), reloids is a generalization of uniform spaces and directed graphs. Funcoid is determined by its action.

Remember that elements of our posets of objects may be such things as sets or more generally filters, they may be not just points. So our topological construction is ``pointfree'' (we may consider sets or filters, not points).

The book is about this topic: describing general topology in terms of
ordered semigroup and ordered semicategory actions.  Funcoids, reloids,

Semiordered semicategory action is \emph{ordered by elements} when \[ a\sqsubseteq b \Leftarrow \supfun{a}\sqsubseteq \supfun{b} \] that is when \[ a\sqsubseteq b \Leftarrow \forall x:\supfun{a}x\sqsubseteq\supfun{b}x. \]

Obviously, in this case~$\supfun{}$ is a faithful functor. So our ordered semicategory action is \emph{essentially functional} (functional, up to a faithful functor).

\chapter{Morphisms of ordered semicategory actions}

\begin{defn}
The \emph{category of semicategories} is the category whose objects are semicategories and whose morphisms are semifunctors.
\end{defn}

\begin{defn}
Let $A:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbf{Set}$ and $B:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbf{Set}$ be semicategory actions. A \emph{morphism of semicategory actions} is a pair $(f,g)$ where
\begin{enumerate}
\item $f:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}$ is a semifunctor of the underlying semicategories;
\item for each object $X\in\Ob\mathcal{C}$, $g_X:A_X\to B_{f(X)}$ is a map;
\end{enumerate}
subject to the compatibility condition
\[
g_Y\bigl(\supfun{a}(x)\bigr)=\supfun{f(a)}'\bigl(g_X(x)\bigr)
\]
for every morphism $a:X\to Y$ and every $x\in A_X$.
\end{defn}

\begin{defn}
\emph{The category of semicategory actions} is the category whose objects are semicategory actions and whose morphisms are morphisms of semicategory actions.
\end{defn}

The compatibility condition is exactly the compact form
\[
g_Y\circ\supfun{a}=\supfun{f(a)}'\circ g_X
\]
for every morphism $a:X\to Y$.

Now suppose the underlying sets of the actions are posets and the semicategories are ordered:

Let $(\mathcal{C},\sqsubseteq,\supfun{})$ and $(\mathcal{D},\sqsubseteq',\supfun{}')$ be ordered semicategory actions. The natural notion of morphism is the one that preserves the underlying semicategory, the order, and the action itself.

Then $(f,g)$ is a morphism of \emph{ordered semicategory actions} if, in addition, $f$ is monotone on morphisms and every component $g_X$ is an order homomorphism.

\begin{defn}
A \emph{homomorphism of ordered semicategory actions} is a morphism $(f,g)$ of the underlying semicategory actions such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $f$ is monotone on morphisms, that is $a\sqsubseteq b\Rightarrow f(a)\sqsubseteq'f(b)$;
\item each component $g_X$ is monotone.
\fxnote{It is natural to also consider the cases when only one of two above axioms is satisfied.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{defn}

\section{Isomorphisms}

\begin{obvious}
An isomorphism of semicategories~$\mathcal{C}$ and~$\mathcal{D}$ is a semifunctor $f:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}$ that has a two-sided inverse semifunctor $f^{-1}:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}$, that is $f\circ f^{-1}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{D}}$ and $f^{-1}\circ f=\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{C}}$.
\end{obvious}

\begin{prop}
Let $(f,g)$ be data such that
\begin{enumerate}
\item $f:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}$ is a semifunctor of the underlying semicategories;
\item ${\supfun{}}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbf{Set}$ and ${\supfun{}}':\mathcal{D}\to\mathbf{Set}$ are ordered semicategory actions;
\item for each object $X\in\Ob\mathcal{C}$, $g_X:\supfun{X}\to \supfun{f(X)}'$ is a map;
\end{enumerate}
the following are pairwise equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $(f,g)$ is an isomorphism of ordered semicategory actions;
\item
\begin{enumerate}
\item $(f,g)$~is a morphism of ordered semicategory actions;
\item $f:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}$ is an isomorphism of semicategories;
\item $f$~is an order isomorphism on every hom-set;
\item every $g_X$ is an
order isomorphism;
\end{enumerate}
\item
\begin{enumerate}
\item $f:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}$ is an isomorphism of semicategories;
\item $f$~is an order isomorphism on every hom-set of~$\mathcal{C}$;
\item every $g_X$ is an
order isomorphism;
\item for every morphism $a:X\to Y$ and every $x\in\supfun{X}$,
\[
g_Y\bigl(\supfun{a}(x)\bigr)
 =\supfun{f(a)}'\bigl(g_X(x)\bigr),
\]
in other words, $g_Y\circ\supfun{a}=\supfun{f(a)}'\circ g_X$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Suppose first that $(f,g)$ is an isomorphism, and denote its inverse by
$(h,k)$.  The equalities
\[
(h,k)\circ(f,g)=\id
\quad\text{and}\quad
(f,g)\circ(h,k)=\id
\]
show that $h=f^{-1}$, and that $k_{f(X)}=g_X^{-1}$ for every
$X\in\Ob\mathcal C$.  Thus $f$ is an isomorphism of semicategories and
every $g_X$ is an order isomorphism.  Since both $f$ and $h$ are monotone
on morphisms, the restriction
\[
f:\Hom_{\mathcal C}(X,Y)\longrightarrow
  \Hom_{\mathcal D}(f(X),f(Y))
\]
and its inverse are monotone; hence this restriction is an order
isomorphism.  This proves the second assertion.

The second and third assertions are equivalent merely by expanding the
definition of a morphism of ordered semicategory actions (including its
compatibility with the actions).

It remains to prove directly that the third assertion gives an inverse.
Put $h=f^{-1}$ and, for each $Z\in\Ob\mathcal D$, define
\[
k_Z=(g_{h(Z)})^{-1}:\supfun{Z}'\longrightarrow\supfun{h(Z)}.
\]
The maps $h$ on hom-sets and $k_Z$ are monotone because they are inverses
of order isomorphisms.  If $b:Z\to W$ is a morphism of $\mathcal D$, apply
the compatibility condition to $a=h(b)$ and then compose with the
appropriate inverses.  We obtain
\[
k_W\circ\supfun{b}'=\supfun{h(b)}\circ k_Z.
\]
Consequently $(h,k)$ is a morphism of ordered semicategory actions.
By its definition, its two composites with $(f,g)$ are the respective
identity morphisms.  Therefore $(f,g)$ is an isomorphism.
\end{proof}

The morphisms for (ordered) semigroup actions are defined similarly, with the only difference that the underlying semicategories have just one object.

\section{Equality and isomorphism of representations}

Equality of morphisms of ordered semicategory actions is componentwise:
if $(f,g)$ and $(f',g')$ have the same source and destination, then
\[
(f,g)=(f',g')\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
f=f'\ \land\ \forall X\in\Ob\mathcal C:g_X=g'_X.
\]
Thus it retains both the morphism of the underlying ordered
semicategories and the maps constituting its action component.

\fxwarning{Move it below these terms are defined,}
There is an important distinction of categorical level.  A pointfree
funcoid, funcoid, reloid, or MGNRM is a morphism \emph{in the underlying
ordered semicategory}.  It is not, merely by that fact, a morphism
$(f,g)$ \emph{between ordered semicategory actions}: in the latter,
$f$ is a semifunctor between two entire semicategories and $g$ is a family
indexed by all objects of its source.  Consequently the phrase
``the corresponding morphism of ordered semicategory actions'' requires a
separately defined correspondence; the action $a\mapsto\supfun{a}$ alone
does not define one.

Denote the category of ordered semicategory actions by~$\mathbf{OSA}$.
To state the exact general result, recall that the arrow category
$\operatorname{Arr}(\mathcal E)$ of a category~$\mathcal E$ has the
morphisms of~$\mathcal E$ as objects and commutative squares as morphisms.
Thus isomorphism in $\operatorname{Arr}(\mathcal E)$ is precisely
isomorphism of morphisms by isomorphisms of their sources and
destinations.

\begin{thm}\label{representation-by-osa-morphisms}
Let~$\mathcal K$ be a category of structures and let
\[
R:\mathcal K\longrightarrow
\operatorname{Arr}(\mathbf{OSA})
\]
be a representation of its objects by morphisms of ordered semicategory
actions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Objects of~$\mathcal K$ are equal if and only if their corresponding
morphisms of ordered semicategory actions are equal exactly when the
object map of~$R$ is injective.
\item Isomorphic objects have isomorphic corresponding morphisms whenever
$R$ is a functor.
\item If $R$ is fully faithful, $R(A)\cong R(B)$ implies $A\cong B$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
The first item is the definition of injectivity of the object map. A
functor preserves inverse morphisms, proving the second item. For the
third, suppose $R(A)\cong R(B)$. By full faithfulness, an isomorphism
$R(A)\to R(B)$ and its inverse lift to morphisms $u:A\to B$ and
$v:B\to A$.  Faithfulness applied to their two composites shows
$v\circ u=\id_A$ and $u\circ v=\id_B$.  Hence $A\cong B$.
\end{proof}

We now construct the required correspondence.  If~$\mathcal C$ is an
ordered semicategory, let~$\widehat{\mathcal C}$ denote its unital
completion.  The order of~$\mathcal C$ is retained, and each newly adjoined
identity is comparable only with itself.  Let
$\mathbf{OSA}_{\widehat{\mathcal C}}$ be the category of ordered
semicategory actions on the fixed ordered category
$\widehat{\mathcal C}$ and action morphisms whose underlying semifunctor
is $\id_{\widehat{\mathcal C}}$.

For every object~$X$ define the ordered semicategory action
\[
\mathsf H_X:\widehat{\mathcal C}\longrightarrow\mathbf{Pos},
\qquad
\mathsf H_X(Z)=\Hom_{\widehat{\mathcal C}}(X,Z),
\]
where
\[
\mathsf H_X(b)(u)=b\circ u.
\]
For every interspace $a:X\to Y$ define
\[
\eta^a:\mathsf H_Y\longrightarrow\mathsf H_X,
\qquad
\eta^a_Z(u)=u\circ a.
\]
Thus the corresponding morphism of ordered semicategory actions is
$(\id_{\widehat{\mathcal C}},\eta^a)$.

\begin{thm}[Ordered Yoneda correspondence]
For all objects $X,Y$ of~$\widehat{\mathcal C}$, the assignment
\[
a\longmapsto\eta^a
\]
is a bijection
\[
\Hom_{\widehat{\mathcal C}}(X,Y)
\longrightarrow
\Hom_{\mathbf{OSA}_{\widehat{\mathcal C}}}
   (\mathsf H_Y,\mathsf H_X).
\]
Consequently it is a fully faithful contravariant representation.
It induces a fully faithful contravariant functor on arrow categories,
and hence two interspaces are equal, respectively isomorphic as arrows,
if and only if their corresponding morphisms of ordered semicategory
actions are equal, respectively isomorphic in
$\operatorname{Arr}(\mathbf{OSA}_{\widehat{\mathcal C}})$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Composition in an ordered semicategory is monotone in both arguments.
Therefore every $\mathsf H_X(b)$ and every $\eta^a_Z$ is monotone.
Moreover, $b\sqsubseteq c$ implies
$\mathsf H_X(b)(u)\sqsubseteq\mathsf H_X(c)(u)$ for every~$u$.
Associativity gives
\[
\mathsf H_X(c)\mathsf H_X(b)(u)=(c\circ b)\circ u
 =\mathsf H_X(c\circ b)(u),
\]
so $\mathsf H_X$ is an ordered semicategory action.  It also gives
\[
\eta^a_W\mathsf H_Y(b)(u)=(b\circ u)\circ a
 =b\circ(u\circ a)=\mathsf H_X(b)\eta^a_Z(u),
\]
so $\eta^a$ is a morphism of such actions.

Let $\theta:\mathsf H_Y\to\mathsf H_X$ be any morphism in
$\mathbf{OSA}_{\widehat{\mathcal C}}$ and put
\[
a=\theta_Y(\id_Y)\in\Hom_{\widehat{\mathcal C}}(X,Y).
\]
Naturality with respect to $u:Y\to Z$ yields
\[
\theta_Z(u)=\theta_Z(u\circ\id_Y)
 =u\circ\theta_Y(\id_Y)=u\circ a=\eta^a_Z(u).
\]
Thus $\theta=\eta^a$, and $a$ is unique because
$a=\eta^a_Y(\id_Y)$.  This proves the displayed bijection.  Moreover
\[
\eta^{b\circ a}=\eta^a\circ\eta^b,
\qquad
\eta^{\id_X}=\id_{\mathsf H_X},
\]
which proves contravariant functoriality and full faithfulness.
The object map is injective: if $\mathsf H_X=\mathsf H_Y$, then
$\id_X\in\mathsf H_Y(X)$ forces $X=Y$.
Applying a fully faithful functor to commutative squares gives a fully
faithful functor between the corresponding arrow categories. Here that
functor is also injective on objects, so it preserves and reflects
equality as well as isomorphism.
\end{proof}

\begin{cor}
Apply the preceding theorem to each of the ordered semicategories of
pointfree funcoids, funcoids, reloids, and MGNRMs.  In every one of these
four cases the formula
\[
a\longmapsto(\id,\eta^a),
\qquad \eta^a_Z(u)=u\circ a,
\]
defines the promised correspondence with morphisms of ordered
semicategory actions.  Two structures of the same kind are equal if and
only if the corresponding action morphisms are equal, and they are
isomorphic as arrows if and only if the corresponding action morphisms
are isomorphic in the fixed-base arrow category.
\end{cor}

\begin{proof}
Each of the four kinds is an interspace in its ordered semicategory, so
the ordered Yoneda construction applies.  The conclusions are exactly
the equality and isomorphism conclusions of the preceding theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{rem}
Equality and isomorphism are both important.  Equality is the first
technical requirement: injectivity says that the representation loses no
distinction made by the original theory.  Isomorphism is normally more
important for classification, because it ignores a change of coordinates.
Accordingly, a useful representation should preferably be both injective
on objects and fully faithful; neither requirement replaces the other.
\end{rem}

\begin{rem}
The same definition works for the dagger case: a morphism of ordered dagger semicategory actions is required to commute with the involution, that is $f(a^{\dagger})=f(a)^{\dagger}$ and $g\bigl(\supfun{a^{\dagger}}x\bigr)=\supfun{f(a)^{\dagger}}'g(x)$ whenever the formula is meaningful. The next chapter develops the corresponding ordered dagger framework.
\end{rem}

\section{Full representations and the catalogue of spaces-in-general}
\label{sec-space-in-general-representation}

\begin{defn}
Let $\mathcal K$ be a category of structures with specified morphisms.
A \emph{categorical representation by ordered actions} is a specified
isomorphism of categories
\[
 R:\mathcal K\xrightarrow{\ \cong\ }\mathcal S,
 \qquad \mathcal S\subseteq\operatorname{Arr}(\mathbf{OSA})
 \text{ a full subcategory}.
\]
A \emph{space-in-general} in this sense consists of an object
$X\in\mathcal K$ equipped with such a representation. Its representative
is the arrow $R(X)$. Equivalently, one specifies a functor to
$\operatorname{Arr}(\mathbf{OSA})$ that is full, faithful, and injective on
objects. For two objects in the same represented category, this gives
\begin{equation}\label{space-in-general-isomorphism-test}
 X\cong Y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad R(X)\cong R(Y).
\end{equation}
Every isomorphism on the right is induced by a unique isomorphism on the
left, because the functor is fully faithful.
\end{defn}

\begin{lem}\label{lem-osa-diagram-embedding}
Let $D$ be a small category. The category $\mathbf{Set}^{D}$ of
set-valued functors and natural transformations is isomorphic to a full
subcategory of $\mathbf{OSA}$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
For a functor $A:D\to\mathbf{Set}$ form the single-sorted algebra with
carrier
\[
 \{0,1\}\sqcup\coprod_{d\in\Ob D} A(d).
\]
The summands are tagged. Use two unary constant operations with values
$0$ and $1$ to name the extra elements. For each $d$ give
the algebra a unary operation that takes the value $1$ precisely on the
summand for $d$ and the value $0$ elsewhere. For each arrow $a:d\to e$
give it a unary operation that acts as $A(a)$ from the $d$-summand to
the $e$-summand and is constantly $0$ elsewhere. This is one fixed
finitary signature, with a set of operation symbols. Every algebra
homomorphism fixes $0,1$ and preserves the summands. Commutation with the
arrow operations is exactly the naturality of its restrictions. Every
natural transformation extends uniquely by fixing $0,1$. The algebra
also determines $A$, since the summands and arrow maps can be recovered
from its labelled operations.

Use the classical full embedding of the category of algebras of a
fixed finitary signature into semigroups (Sichler's
corollary~\cite{sichler-semigroup-embedding}). Here the embedding is
injective on objects. Send a semigroup to its one-object ordered
semicategory with equality order, acting on the one-point poset. Between
two such actions, the poset component of a morphism is unique and the
underlying semifunctor is exactly an arbitrary semigroup homomorphism.
This last functor is also full, faithful, and injective on objects.
\end{proof}

\begin{thm}\label{thm-osa-full-embedding}
Every small category $\mathcal K$ is isomorphic to a full subcategory of
$\mathbf{OSA}$ and to a full subcategory of
$\operatorname{Arr}(\mathbf{OSA})$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Yoneda sends $X\in\mathcal K$ to
$\Hom_{\mathcal K}(-,X)\in\mathbf{Set}^{\mathcal K^{\mathrm{op}}}$ and
sends an arrow to postcomposition by that arrow. A natural
transformation out of $\Hom_{\mathcal K}(-,X)$ is determined by its value
on $\id_X$ at $X$. If its target is $\Hom_{\mathcal K}(-,Y)$ that value
is an arrow $X\to Y$, which induces the transformation. This proves
full faithfulness. Using typed arrows, equality of two representables
implies equality of their representing objects: evaluate at $X$ and
use the fact that $\id_X$ has target $X$. Now apply
Lemma~\ref{lem-osa-diagram-embedding}.

For any category $\mathcal E$, the functor
$\mathcal E\to\operatorname{Arr}(\mathcal E)$ sending $A$ to $\id_A$
is full, faithful, and injective on objects. In a commutative square
between identity arrows, the two components must be the same arrow
$A\to B$. Apply this functor with $\mathcal E=\mathbf{OSA}$.
\end{proof}

The two target categories have different objects and morphisms. In
$\mathbf{OSA}$ an object is an action and a morphism is an action
homomorphism. In $\operatorname{Arr}(\mathbf{OSA})$ an object is an action
homomorphism and a morphism is a commuting square. The identity-arrow
embedding gives one way to pass from the first representation to the
second. For existence of full representations of small categories,
Theorem~\ref{thm-osa-full-embedding} gives both targets.

With the definitions here the two target categories are inequivalent.
An object is subterminal if it receives at most one morphism from each
object. A subterminal action has at most one base object, at most one arrow,
and a carrier of at most one element at its sole base object, if any.
To see the three bounds, test maps out of the arrowless one-object base
with empty carrier, the free one-generator semigroup with empty carrier,
and the arrowless one-object base with singleton carrier, respectively.
Conversely, each action satisfying the three bounds is subterminal.
This gives five isomorphism classes: the empty action and the four
one-object actions obtained by independently allowing or omitting the
single arrow and the single carrier element. In the arrow category the
five identity arrows on these actions are subterminal, as is the arrow
from the empty action to the terminal one. That arrow is in a sixth
isomorphism class, since it has nonisomorphic endpoints.

The smallness condition is relative to the ambient category. For
example, take all structures and maps small in a universe $\mathcal U$,
and carry out the theorem in a larger universe containing $\mathcal U$.
Then each standard category in the catalogue below is small for the
proof, even if it is large in $\mathcal U$. One can also work with a
specified set of structures and a set of maps in ordinary set theory.
The theorem makes no assertion about representing all sizes at once
using only actions small in the original universe.

\subsection{Algebraic and relational structures}
\label{sec-osa-algebraic-structures}

There is also a direct application of
Lemma~\ref{lem-osa-diagram-embedding} which uses just the signature.
For a fixed set-sized signature, take a diagram with one set for each
sort. For each operation, add a set of input tuples with its coordinate
projections and a map giving the output. A nullary operation has the
singleton of empty tuples as its input sort. For each relation, add its
set of tuples with the projections to its argument sorts. The index
category is the free category on this fixed diagram of named arrows.

In a natural transformation of these diagrams, the components on tuple
sorts are forced coordinatewise by the projections. On a relation sort
the required tuple must belong to the target relation. On an operation
sort the output map forces the ordinary homomorphism equation. Conversely,
any family of homomorphisms of the underlying sorts that respects the
operations and relations extends by these coordinatewise formulas.
The diagrams determine the original structure, including its labels.
This gives an injective-on-objects, fully faithful representation for
algebraic and relational structures with their usual homomorphisms. It
works with set-sized arities when the requisite tuple sets are allowed.
It also applies to full subcategories specified by axioms on the
structures, such as groups, rings, or vector spaces over a fixed field.

Thus every ordinary set-based algebraic structure admits a
space-in-general representation. The existence of the categorical
isomorphism cannot distinguish a familiar geometric or topological space
from an arbitrary algebra. The definition records a choice of $R$ and
retains the represented structure and its maps. The universal construction
alone supplies no particular formula for closure, distance, or
continuity. To obtain such a formula one studies a representation with
specific ordered actions, as in the closure example and the later
chapters on interspaces.

\subsection{Graphs and relations}
\label{sec-osa-graphs}

A simple undirected graph on $X$ is precisely an irreflexive symmetric
relation $R\subseteq X\times X$. In the ordered dagger category
$\mathbf{Rel}$, this says that $R:X\to X$ satisfies
$R=R^{\dagger}$ and $R\cap\id_X=\varnothing$. If loops are permitted,
omit the irreflexivity condition. An arbitrary endorelation gives a
directed graph with loops allowed, with at most one edge per ordered
pair. Relational image gives the ordered action of $\mathbf{Rel}$ on
powersets, so an adjacency relation is directly an interspace of that
action. Regarding vertex maps as their graph relations, the graph
homomorphism condition is
\[
 f\circ R\subseteq S\circ f\qquad(f:X\to Y).
\]
Indeed, for each $xRx'$ it requires $f(x)S f(x')$. A graph isomorphism
is a bijection with $f\circ R=S\circ f$.

A square in $\operatorname{Arr}(\mathbf{Rel})$ may use independent
source and target maps even for two symmetric endorelations. This changes
the isomorphism relation. For example, on
$\{0,1,2\}\times\{0,1\}$ let $R$ join pairs with different first
coordinates and the same second coordinate, and let $S$ join pairs
with different first coordinates and different second coordinates.
Then $R$ gives two disjoint triangles and $S$ gives a six-cycle.
Flipping the second coordinate at the target gives an arrow
isomorphism, since for that flip $t$ we have $t\circ R=S$. The graphs
have different numbers of connected components.

To recover graph maps in a full representation, use one vertex sort
and one edge-relation sort, with two projections to the \emph{same}
vertex sort. A natural transformation has a single vertex component.
Its edge component is forced by the projections and exists precisely for
a graph homomorphism. The diagram lemma gives the claimed full embedding
for directed and undirected simple graphs. A directed multigraph has an
independent edge set and two endpoint maps. For undirected multigraphs
one may use an incidence diagram of vertices, edges, and ends, retaining
both incidences at a loop. Hypergraphs with specified incidence maps
likewise have an incidence diagram. In any convention the morphisms must
be specified; for example, a map of incidence diagrams includes the
maps on its incidence data.

\subsection{Catalogue of spaces}

Here are the principal families to which the representation theorem

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Graphs, hypergraphs, relations, and functions.}
Use the graph and incidence representations above. With binary relations

\item \emph{General topology and pointfree topology.}
Topological, preclosure, pretopological, proximity, uniform, Cauchy,

\item \emph{Metric, linear, and geometric spaces.}
Metric spaces with nonexpansive maps, or with distance-preserving maps.
For the former, the binary relations $d(x,y)\le r$ for nonnegative

labelled relations as well. The same distance presentation applies

Measurable spaces with measurable maps, measure and probability

Pointfree funcoids, funcoids, reloids, and MGNRMs can be taken as objects

\chapter{Ordered dagger categories and ordered semigroups with involution}

\begin{defn}
\emph{Dagger semicategory} is a semicategory together with the operation $a\mapsto a^{\dagger}$ (called \emph{involution} or \emph{dagger}) such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $a^{\dagger\dagger} = a$;
\item $(b\circ a)^{\dagger} = a^{\dagger}\circ b^{\dagger}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{defn}

For an \emph{ordered dagger semicategory} we additionally require
\[
 a\sqsubseteq b\Rightarrow a^{\dagger}\sqsubseteq b^{\dagger}
\]
(and hence $a\sqsubseteq b\Leftrightarrow a^{\dagger}\sqsubseteq b^{\dagger}$).

\begin{defn}
\emph{Semigroup with involution} is a dagger semicategory with just one object.
\end{defn}

For an \emph{ordered semigroup with involution} or \emph{ordered dagger semigroup} we likewise require monotonicity of the involution:
\[
 a\sqsubseteq b\Rightarrow a^{\dagger}\sqsubseteq b^{\dagger}
\quad\text{and hence}\quad
 a\sqsubseteq b\Leftrightarrow a^{\dagger}\sqsubseteq b^{\dagger}.
\]

A prototypical example is the semigroup of binary relations on a set under composition, with the dagger given by converse relation. In such an example, the order is inclusion and the involution is order preserving. This is why the formulas from the preceding chapter with $f^{\dagger}$ are the correct generalization of the usual adjoint or inverse operations.

\begin{defn}
An \emph{ordered dagger semicategory action} is an ordered semicategory action $\supfun{}$ together with an involution on the underlying semicategory and a compatible involution on the values of the action, such that
\[
\supfun{a^{\dagger}} = \bigl(\supfun{a}\bigr)^{\dagger}
\]
whenever the latter is defined.
\end{defn}

This compatibility means that the action preserves the dagger operation and thus the formulas
\[
\mathrm{C}(a,b)\Leftrightarrow f\circ a\sqsubseteq b\circ f,
\qquad
\mathrm{C}'(a,b)\Leftrightarrow a\sqsubseteq f^{\dagger}\circ b\circ f,
\qquad
\mathrm{C}''(a,b)\Leftrightarrow f\circ a\circ f^{\dagger}\sqsubseteq b
\]
are the natural generalizations of continuity in ordered dagger settings. In particular, the involution allows us to express the inverse or adjoint direction of a morphism without leaving the algebraic framework.

\begin{rem}
For ordered semigroup actions with involution, the same definitions reduce to the one-object case. Then the involution on the semigroup and the action are compatible, and the whole theory becomes the algebraic language of symmetry, duality, and generalized continuity.
\end{rem}

\section{Transformation groups}

If a group~$G$ acts faithfully on a set~$X$, let
\[
 \mathcal A(X,G)=(G,=,\subsets X,\supfun{}),\qquad
 \supfun{g}A=\rsupfun{g}A.
\]
Thus the order on~$G$ is equality, the order on~$\subsets X$ is inclusion,
and every group element acts by direct image.  This is an ordered monoid
action (and hence an OSA).  It is preferable here to a single orbit
operator on sets of sets: the latter may forget which motion produced a
given image.

\begin{lem}
An isomorphism
$\mathcal A(X,G)\cong\mathcal A(Y,H)$ is exactly a pair consisting of a
group isomorphism~$\alpha:G\to H$ and a bijection~$h:X\to Y$ such that
\[
 h\circ g=\alpha(g)\circ h\quad(g\in G).
\]
In other words, this OSA remembers the permutation group, up to conjugacy.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
An order isomorphism $\subsets X\to\subsets Y$ sends atoms to atoms, so it
has the form $A\mapsto h[A]$ for a unique bijection~$h:X\to Y$.  The
compatibility axiom for an isomorphism of actions, applied to singleton
sets, is precisely the displayed equation.  The converse is immediate.
\end{proof}

\section{Euclidean spaces}

\begin{prop}
Let $E$ and~$F$ be finite-dimensional real Euclidean spaces, and put
$G_E=\operatorname{Isom}(E)$ and $G_F=\operatorname{Isom}(F)$.  Then
\[
 E\cong F\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \mathcal A(E,G_E)\cong\mathcal A(F,G_F).
\]
Here the isomorphism on the left is a Euclidean isometry.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
An isometry $h:E\to F$ conjugates $G_E$ onto~$G_F$, so it induces the OSA
isomorphism $A\mapsto h[A]$.

Conversely, the lemma gives a bijection $h:E\to F$ conjugating the two full
motion groups. For distinct $x,y$, the line through them is the common
fixed set of the isometries fixing both points. Every such isometry fixes
the line pointwise, and the isometry that negates the orthogonal complement
of the line fixes precisely the line. This description includes dimension
one, when the line is the whole space. Conjugation therefore carries lines
to lines. Closing a nonempty set under the lines through pairs of its
points gives its affine span: after translating a point of the set to
the origin, closure under lines gives scalar multiples and sums (take
the midpoint and double it). Consequently $h$ preserves affine
independence and dimension. Dimension zero is detected by having one
point. Equal finite Euclidean dimensions give an isometry by choosing
orthonormal coordinates.
\end{proof}

This result concerns isomorphism classes, but the construction is not fully
faithful on morphisms.  For example, in dimension at least two every
similarity conjugates the motion group to itself, although a similarity of
ratio different from one is not an isometry.  On the real line there are
still more action automorphisms, coming from wild additive automorphisms of
$(\mathbb R,+)$.  Thus the motion-group OSA recovers a Euclidean space only
up to its isomorphism class, not its individual Euclidean morphisms.

\section{Affine spaces}

For an affine space~$A$, let $P_A$ and~$L_A$ be its sets of points and
lines.  On the disjoint union $P_A\sqcup L_A$, distinguish the identity
relations on $P_A$ and~$L_A$ (which mark the two sorts) and the point--line
incidence relation.  These are elements of the ordered monoid of binary
relations.  This monoid acts on the powerset by relational image, so the
distinguished relations form a pointed OSA.

\begin{prop}
Two affine spaces of dimension at least two have isomorphic pointed OSAs of
the above kind if and only if they are isomorphic as affine incidence
spaces.  For affine spaces over division rings, such an isomorphism is
induced by a semiaffine bijection.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
An affine-space isomorphism preserves points, lines, and incidence, so it
induces an isomorphism of the pointed OSAs.  Conversely, an OSA
isomorphism preserves the two distinguished sorts and the incidence
relation, and therefore restricts to an incidence-preserving bijection of
points.  The fundamental theorem of affine geometry says that, in
dimension at least two, this bijection is semiaffine.
\end{proof}

An affine line is exceptional: its bare point--line incidence structure
does not recover the coordinate division ring.  In dimension one a
coordinatization, or equivalent additional structure, must also be
distinguished.

\section{Vector spaces}

Fix a field~$K$.  For a $K$-vector space~$V$, take the disjoint union
\[
 X_V=V\sqcup(V\times V).
\]
The ordered monoid of all binary relations on~$X_V$ acts on
$\subsets X_V$ by relational image.  In this OSA distinguish the identity
relations on the two summands, the relations
\[
 P_i=\setcond{((x_1,x_2),x_i)}{x_1,x_2\in V}\quad(i=1,2),
 \qquad
 S=\setcond{((x,y),x+y)}{x,y\in V},
\]
and, for every labelled scalar~$a\in K$, the relation
\[
 M_a=\setcond{(x,ax)}{x\in V}.
\]
Call the resulting pointed OSA~$\mathcal V_K(V)$.

\begin{prop}
For any two $K$-vector spaces $V$ and~$W$,
\[
 V\cong_K W\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \mathcal V_K(V)\cong\mathcal V_K(W),
\]
where an isomorphism of the pointed OSAs is required to preserve all the
displayed distinguished relations and their scalar labels.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Let~$H:X_V\to X_W$ be the bijection induced by an isomorphism of the
pointed OSAs.  Preservation of the two distinguished identity relations
implies that~$H$ maps $V$ onto~$W$ and $V\times V$ onto~$W\times W$.  Put
$h=H|_V$.  Preservation of~$P_1$ and~$P_2$ forces
\[
 H(x,y)=(h(x),h(y))qquad(x,y\in V).
\]
Preservation of~$S$ now gives
\[
 h(x+y)=h(x)+h(y),
\]
and preservation of every labelled~$M_a$ gives
\[
 h(ax)=a h(x).
\]
Thus~$h$ is a bijective $K$-linear map, so $V\cong_K W$.

Conversely, a $K$-linear isomorphism~$h:V\to W$ extends to the bijection
$H:X_V\to X_W$ given by $H|_V=h$ and
$H(x,y)=(h(x),h(y))$.  Direct image by~$H$ is an order isomorphism of the
powersets, conjugation by~$H$ is an isomorphism of the ordered relation
monoids, and these maps preserve all the distinguished relations.
Therefore they define an isomorphism
$\mathcal V_K(V)\cong\mathcal V_K(W)$.
\end{proof}

If vector spaces over different fields are to be compared, add the field
as another distinguished sort, together with the required product sorts
and projections, the graphs of field addition and multiplication, and the
scalar-action relation.  The same proof then shows that an isomorphism of
the resulting pointed OSAs is exactly a field isomorphism together with a
semilinear bijection of the vector spaces.
The labelled operations also yield a full representation on linear maps
by Section~\ref{sec-osa-algebraic-structures}, which retains the labels
in its diagram.

\section{Hilbert and Banach spaces}

The norm distance of a Hilbert or Banach space is an MGNRM interspace.
To represent the usual linear isometries, one can use the diagram
construction with one vector sort, its linear operations over the fixed
scalar field, and the labelled unary relations
$\{v:\lVert v\rVert\le r\}$ for nonnegative rational $r$, together with
their complements. Homomorphisms preserve every threshold and its
complement, hence preserve the norm. Their inverses, when they exist, are
linear isometries as well, and every surjective linear isometry gives
such an isomorphism. Keeping only the sublevel sets gives the category
of linear contractions. For a Hilbert space the norm also determines
the inner product by polarization.

The norm labels carry information beyond the bare action of the
isometry group. For example, in a nonzero Hilbert space every radial
bijection
\[
 x\longmapsto
 \begin{cases}
  0,&x=0,\\
  \varphi(\lVert x\rVert)x/\lVert x\rVert,&x\ne0,
 \end{cases}
\]
where $\varphi:\mathbb R_+\to\mathbb R_+$ is a bijection fixing zero,
commutes with every orthogonal (or unitary) operator. Choosing
$\varphi\ne\id$ gives an automorphism of the bare symmetry action that
changes the norm.

\section{Projective spaces}

For a projective space the natural faithful datum is incidence rather than
only its symmetry group.  Take the disjoint union of its points and lines,
distinguish the two sorts, and distinguish the incidence relation as an
element of the ordered monoid of binary relations.  An isomorphism of these
pointed OSAs is exactly an incidence-preserving bijection. For projective
spaces over division rings of dimension at least two, the fundamental
theorem of projective geometry
then identifies it with a semilinear isomorphism (with the corresponding
isomorphism of division rings).  Without distinguishing the two sorts one
may also obtain sort-exchanging dualities, notably for projective planes.
Projective lines are an exception: bare point--line incidence does not
recover the coordinate division ring, so one must retain additional
structure (for example the cross-ratio or a coordinatization).

Thus the motion-group action suffices to classify finite-dimensional
Euclidean spaces up to isometry. For full representations with ordinary
maps, labelled distances, vector operations, and incidence provide
alternative data as appropriate.
